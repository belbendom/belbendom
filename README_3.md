<h2 align="left">Research</h2>
<div align="justify">

Wine fermentation is carried out by diverse communities of yeasts whose composition shapes the chemistry and quality of the resulting wine. This makes it a workable system for a general question in microbial ecology: how the composition of a community sets its function, and how that function can be predicted and engineered. My thesis addresses this using **synthetic wine-yeast communities**, from two complementary directions.

The first applies **directed evolution** to community function. Starting from a fixed set of commercial wine yeasts, I adjust the inoculation dose of each species across successive selection rounds and follow community-level functions such as sugar consumption and lactic acid production. Rebuilding the community from pure cultures at each round keeps the procedure reproducible and moves the study of community-function relationships from a presence/absence view to a quantitative, dose-resolved one.

The second looks inside the community, asking how diversity shapes function at the molecular level. Using synthetic communities along a diversity gradient, with *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* as focal species, I combine **transcriptomic and metabolomic** profiling to read the response at two scales at once, the whole community and the focal species, and to describe how community context reshapes the activity underlying fermentation.

The two lines share a goal: connecting the ecology of fermenting communities with their engineering, so that consortia can be designed for target functions.

</div>
